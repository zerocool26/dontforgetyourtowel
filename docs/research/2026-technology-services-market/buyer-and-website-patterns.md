# Buyer and website patterns

## 1. Lead with the business move, not the technology catalog

Large providers rarely open with every platform, certification, and delivery
method. They lead with a decisive outcome—reinvent, modernize, build, operate,
secure—and let the service architecture explain how.

Application: the homepage should open with a short, forceful promise and one
sentence that names the integrated scope. Detailed capabilities belong lower on
the page and on the services route.

## 2. Sell a continuum of responsibility

Accenture integrates consulting, technology, and operations. IBM joins strategy
and technology with intelligent operations. Capgemini moves from strategy to
applications to operations. Kyndryl uses advisory, implementation, and managed
services.

Application: organize the offer around **Plan, Build, Secure, Run**. This gives
buyers four understandable entry points without presenting four disconnected
companies.

## 3. Make software engineering part of the operating model

EPAM, Cognizant, and Globant describe software as product engineering,
modernization, platforms, and business systems. The promise is not “we write
code”; it is “we turn an operating need into a maintainable production system.”

Application: describe software work through product definition, architecture,
delivery, integration, quality, security, launch, and ownership after launch.

## 4. Put security inside every service

The benchmark firms increasingly frame security as architecture and continuous
operations, not a separate audit performed at the end.

Application: the website should connect identity, access, recovery, cloud
controls, application security, dependency management, and incident readiness.
The trust center should cover both managed environments and software delivery.

## 5. Use a small number of service lanes

The best portfolios are broad, but their top-level navigation stays compressed.
Detail appears progressively through service pages, industries, and proof.

Application: use six customer-facing lanes beneath the four-stage lifecycle:

- Technology advisory
- Software & product engineering
- Cloud & platform modernization
- Cybersecurity & resilience
- Managed IT & workplace
- Data, integration & automation

## 6. Translate scale into operating proof

Global companies use client counts, revenue, large contracts, partner depth, and
case studies because they can substantiate those claims. A smaller firm should
not mimic those numbers.

Application: prove credibility with observable practices instead:

- a named accountable owner;
- a written current-state assessment;
- explicit scope and acceptance criteria;
- architecture and decision records;
- source, credential, and access ownership rules;
- documented recovery and incident paths;
- production handoff and operating documentation;
- clear pricing assumptions and exclusions.

## 7. Keep AI subordinate to the buyer's problem

Most benchmark sites now put AI everywhere. That may reflect their investment
story, but it often weakens clarity for a mid-market buyer who first needs a
working system, secure data, controlled access, and measurable process change.

Application: do not use AI as a title, badge, or generic promise. Discuss
automation or model-enabled workflows only when tied to a governed, specific use
case and a maintainable operating owner.

## 8. Be aggressive through clarity

Strong language does not require unsupported superlatives. The most credible
aggressive position is that fragmented vendors create delays and ambiguity, and
one accountable team will plan, build, secure, and run the work.

Recommended homepage headline:

> Build faster. Run stronger.

Recommended supporting line:

> Technology strategy, custom software, cloud, cybersecurity, and managed IT—one
> accountable Chicago team from first decision through daily operations.

Recommended primary action:

> Put us to work

Recommended secondary action:

> See what we deliver

These lines are forceful, specific, and supportable without inventing proof.
