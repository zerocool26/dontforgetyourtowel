The first month should make the environment easier to understand. It should not feel like a sales handoff where everyone is still guessing who owns what.

## Start with the operating map

A good managed IT onboarding starts by mapping users, devices, Microsoft 365, core applications, network equipment, backups, vendors, licenses, and recurring pain. The goal is not paperwork for its own sake. The goal is to remove ambiguity before a real incident exposes it.

The fastest wins usually come from simple ownership decisions: who resets access, who approves hardware, who handles vendor escalations, what gets patched, and what leaders see each month.

## Stabilize before expanding

Teams often want a giant roadmap immediately. That can help, but only after urgent support gaps and security basics are visible. A strong provider will usually look for:

- Stale admin accounts and weak identity controls
- Unpatched endpoints and unmanaged devices
- Backup coverage that has not been tested
- Email security gaps and risky forwarding rules
- Recurring tickets that point to a broken process

## Make reporting useful

Monthly reporting should show open work, recurring issues, security posture, and the next decisions. If the report is just ticket counts, it is not enough. Leadership needs to know what is improving, what is still exposed, and what budget decision comes next.

The first 30 days should end with a cleaner runbook, a calmer support lane, and a short roadmap that people can actually use.
